# Root-Cause Diagnostics for Species Decline

## Operational Framework

This reference provides a systematic framework for diagnosing the root causes of species and population declines. It operationalizes the HIPPO framework (Habitat loss, Invasive species, Pollution, Population growth, Overexploitation) and other extinction drivers into a structured diagnostic process.

## Diagnostic Philosophy

### Core Principles

**1. Multiple causality is the norm, not the exception**
- Most species declines are driven by multiple threats
- Threats often interact synergistically (e.g., habitat loss increases vulnerability to disease)
- The goal is to identify and rank ALL contributing threats, not just the primary one

**2. Evidence hierarchy matters**
- **Strong evidence:** Direct observation, experimental manipulation, before-after control studies
- **Moderate evidence:** Correlative studies, expert elicitation, modeling results
- **Weak evidence:** Anecdotal reports, inferred from similar species
- **Principle:** Explicitly state evidence strength for each causal inference

**3. Spatial and temporal scale alignment**
- Threat must occur at the right spatial scale to affect the species
- Threat must coincide temporally with the observed decline
- **Principle:** Map threat occurrence onto species' range and decline timeline

**4. Proximate vs. ultimate causes**
- **Proximate cause:** Direct mechanism (e.g., predation by invasive species)
- **Ultimate cause:** Underlying driver (e.g., habitat fragmentation facilitated predator invasion)
- **Principle:** Identify both levels to design effective interventions

## The HIPPO Framework

### H: Habitat Loss and Degradation

**Types:**

**1. Habitat destruction**
- **Conversion:** Transformation to other land uses (agriculture, urban, industrial)
- **Deforestation:** Removal of forest cover
- **Wetland drainage:** Conversion of wetlands to other uses
- **Fragmentation:** Breaking contiguous habitat into smaller, isolated patches

**2. Habitat degradation**
- **Selective logging:** Removing valuable trees without full conversion
- **Overgrazing:** Excessive livestock pressure reducing habitat quality
- **Fire suppression:** Altering fire-dependent ecosystems
- **Pollution impacts:** Reducing habitat suitability (see Pollution section)

**3. Habitat modification**
- **Road construction:** Creating barriers and mortality sources
- **Dam construction:** Altering flow regimes and connectivity
- **Urbanization:** Creating impervious surfaces and edge effects

**Diagnostic questions:**
- Has the extent of suitable habitat declined in the species' range?
- Has habitat quality declined (vegetation structure, resource availability)?
- Has the spatial configuration changed (fragmentation, connectivity)?
- Do land-use change maps correlate with decline hotspots?

**Evidence indicators:**
- Remote sensing data showing habitat loss
- Land-use change maps
- Habitat suitability modeling
- Direct habitat measurements
- Species-habitat relationship studies

### I: Invasive Species

**Types:**

**1. Invasive predators**
- **Direct predation:** Novel predators to which native species have no anti-predator responses
- **Mesopredator release:** Removal of top predators releases mid-level predators
- **Examples:** Rats on islands, cats, snakes (brown tree snake)

**2. Invasive competitors**
- **Resource competition:** Outcompeting natives for food, space, nesting sites
- **Apparent competition:** Shared pathogens increase mortality
- **Examples:** Invasive plants, invasive birds, invasive fish

**3. Invasive ecosystem engineers**
- **Habitat modification:** Altering physical ecosystem structure
- **Examples:** Beavers (outside native range), Tamarisk, Zebra mussels

**4. Invasive pathogens and parasites**
- **Disease introduction:** Novel diseases to which native species have no immunity
- **Examples:** Chytrid fungus in amphibians, White-nose syndrome in bats

**5. Invasive herbivores**
- **Overgrazing/browsing:** Excessive consumption pressure
- **Examples:** Goats, deer, rabbits

**Diagnostic questions:**
- Are invasive species present in the species' range?
- Do invasive species' ranges overlap with decline hotspots?
- Has invasive species arrival coincided temporally with decline onset?
- Are there documented impacts of the invasive species on native species?
- Is there a plausible mechanism (competition, predation, habitat modification, disease)?

**Evidence indicators:**
- Invasive species distribution maps
- Temporal records of invasive species establishment
- Impact studies (experiments, observational studies)
- Diet studies showing overlap
- Co-occurrence patterns

### P: Pollution

**Types:**

**1. Chemical pollution**
- **Pesticides and herbicides:** Direct toxicity, bioaccumulation
- **Industrial chemicals:** Heavy metals, PCBs, dioxins
- **Oil spills:** Acute toxicity and long-term contamination
- **Pharmaceuticals:** Endocrine disruption, behavioral effects

**2. Nutrient pollution**
- **Eutrophication:** Excess nitrogen/phosphorus causing algal blooms, hypoxia
- **Source:** Agricultural runoff, sewage

**3. Plastic pollution**
- **Ingestion:** Species consuming plastic debris
- **Entanglement:** Physical harm from plastic materials
- **Microplastics:** Bioaccumulation and ecosystem effects

**4. Light and noise pollution**
- **Disrupted navigation:** Light pollution affecting insects, birds, sea turtles
- **Communication masking:** Noise pollution affecting acoustic species
- **Stress:** Chronic stress from anthropogenic stimuli

**5. Thermal pollution**
- **Water temperature changes:** From industrial discharges, deforestation of riparian areas
- **Urban heat islands:** Elevated temperatures affecting microclimate

**Diagnostic questions:**
- Are pollution sources present in the species' range?
- Do pollution hotspots correlate with decline hotspots?
- Is the species sensitive to the specific pollutants present?
- Have pollution levels increased over the period of decline?
- Are there documented toxic effects on the species or close relatives?

**Evidence indicators:**
- Pollution monitoring data
- Toxicity studies
- Bioaccumulation measurements
- Tissue contaminant analyses
- Spatial correlation analyses

### P: Population Growth (Human)

**Mechanisms:**

**1. Direct habitat conversion**
- **Agriculture expansion:** Converting natural habitat to cropland or pasture
- **Urban expansion:** Housing, infrastructure, commercial development
- **Resource extraction:** Mining, logging, fishing

**2. Resource consumption**
- **Water withdrawals:** Reducing streamflow, drying wetlands
- **Groundwater depletion:** Lowering water tables
- **Fuelwood collection:** Removing woody biomass

**3. Human-wildlife conflict**
- **Retaliatory killing:** Perceived threats to livestock or crops
- **Vehicle collisions:** Roadkill
- **Pet trade:** Collection for pets

**4. Disturbance**
- **Recreational pressure:** Hiking, camping, wildlife viewing
- **Infrastructure presence:** Roads, power lines, fences creating barriers

**Diagnostic questions:**
- Has human population density increased in the species' range?
- Have human land uses expanded or intensified?
- Do human population centers or activity hotspots overlap with decline hotspots?
- Are there documented conflicts between human activities and the species?

**Evidence indicators:**
- Human population census data
- Land-use change maps
- Infrastructure development records
- Conflict incident reports
- Spatial correlation analyses

### O: Overexploitation

**Types:**

**1. Overhunting/overfishing**
- **Direct harvest:** Exceeding sustainable yield
- **Bycatch:** Incidental mortality during harvest of other species
- **Illegal take:** Poaching, unregulated harvest

**2. Overcollection**
- **Pet trade:** Collection for the exotic pet market
- **Traditional medicine:** Collection for medicinal use
- **Ornamental trade:** Collection for display (corals, shells)

**3. Timber overharvest**
- **Selective logging:** Removing valuable trees
- **Clear-cutting:** Removing all trees

**Diagnostic questions:**
- Is the species subject to legal or illegal harvest?
- Have harvest rates increased over the period of decline?
- Does harvest effort correlate with decline hotspots?
- Are there population models indicating unsustainable take?
- Is there evidence of poaching or illegal trade?

**Evidence indicators:**
- Harvest records (legal and illegal)
- Trade data (CITES records, market surveys)
- Population modeling results
- Effort data (fishing effort, hunting effort)
- Market price data (increasing price can indicate scarcity)

## Additional Threat Categories

### C: Climate Change

**Mechanisms:**

**1. Range shifts**
- **Poleward and upward:** Species moving to track suitable climate
- **Mismatch with resources:** Phenological mismatches with food resources
- **Range contraction:** Species unable to disperse to suitable climate

**2. Extreme events**
- **Droughts:** Increased frequency and severity
- **Heatwaves:** Exceeding thermal tolerances
- **Storms:** Increased intensity
- **Floods:** Altered hydrological regimes

**3. Ocean changes**
- **Warming:** Sea surface temperature increases
- **Acidification:** Reduced pH affecting calcifying organisms
- **Deoxygenation:** Expanding oxygen minimum zones
- **Sea level rise:** Coastal inundation

**4. Phenological changes**
- **Timing mismatches:** Breeding, migration, flowering timing shifts
- **Trophic mismatches:** Predator-prey asynchrony

**Diagnostic questions:**
- Has climate changed in the species' range over the decline period?
- Are climate variables within or beyond the species' thermal/precipitation tolerance?
- Have extreme events increased in frequency or severity?
- Are there documented range shifts or phenological changes?
- Are climate projections suitable for the species?

**Evidence indicators:**
- Climate data (temperature, precipitation, extreme events)
- Species distribution models (current and future)
- Phenological records
- Physiological tolerance studies
- Climate attribution studies

### D: Disease

**Types:**

**1. Novel pathogens**
- **Introduced diseases:** Pathogens introduced from other regions
- **Spillover:** Pathogens jumping from domestic to wild species
- **Examples:** Chytrid, White-nose syndrome, Avian malaria

**2. Emerging infectious diseases**
- **Range expansions:** Pathogens expanding into new areas
- **Host shifts:** Pathogens adapting to new hosts
- **Increased virulence:** Environmental stressors increasing pathogen impact

**Diagnostic questions:**
- Have disease outbreaks been documented in the species?
- Have novel pathogens been introduced to the species' range?
- Does disease prevalence correlate with decline hotspots?
- Are there documented mortality events from disease?
- Are species showing signs of immunosuppression?

**Evidence indicators:**
- Disease surveillance data
- Pathogen detection (molecular, histological)
- Mortality event investigations
- Serological surveys
- Experimental infection studies

### G: Genetic Issues

**Mechanisms:**

**1. Inbreeding depression**
- **Reduced fitness:** Lower survival, fecundity, or growth
- **Expression of deleterious recessive alleles**
- **Loss of heterozygosity**

**2. Outbreeding depression**
- **Reduced fitness:** From breaking up co-adapted gene complexes
- **Occurs:** When genetically distinct populations hybridize

**3. Genetic drift**
- **Loss of genetic diversity:** Especially in small populations
- **Fixation of deleterious alleles**

**Diagnostic questions:**
- Is the population small and isolated (facilitating inbreeding)?
- Have populations recently hybridized with genetically distinct stocks?
- Is there evidence of reduced fitness (survival, fecundity)?
- Are there documented genetic issues in the species or close relatives?

**Evidence indicators:**
- Genetic diversity measurements (heterozygosity, allelic richness)
- Inbreeding coefficient estimates
- Fitness measurements
- Hybridization assessments
- Pedigree analyses

## Step-by-Step Diagnostic Process

### Step 1: Characterize the Decline Pattern

**Describe what you observe:**
- **Temporal pattern:** When did the decline begin? Is it gradual or sudden? Continuous or episodic?
- **Spatial pattern:** Where is the decline most severe? Is it uniform across the range or localized?
- **Demographic pattern:** Which life stages are most affected? Is the decline affecting all subpopulations?
- **Magnitude:** What is the rate and extent of decline?

**Output template:**
```
## Decline Pattern Characterization
- **Temporal pattern:** [Description with timeline]
- **Spatial pattern:** [Description with map if available]
- **Demographic pattern:** [Description of which life stages/sexes affected]
- **Magnitude:** [Quantitative estimate if available]
```

### Step 2: Inventory Potential Threats

**For each threat category (HIPPO + C, D, G), answer:**
- Is this threat present in the species' range?
- When did it appear or intensify?
- How severe is it?
- Does it overlap spatially with the decline?

**Output template:**
```
## Threat Inventory
| Threat Category | Present? | Intensity | Spatial Overlap | Temporal Overlap |
|----------------|----------|-----------|-----------------|-----------------|
| Habitat Loss | [Y/N] | [High/Med/Low] | [Extent] | [Timeframe] |
| Invasive Species | [Y/N] | [High/Med/Low] | [Extent] | [Timeframe] |
| Pollution | [Y/N] | [High/Med/Low] | [Extent] | [Timeframe] |
| [Continue table for all categories] |
```

### Step 3: Evaluate Causal Evidence

**For each threat present, assess:**
- **Evidence strength:** Strong, moderate, or weak?
- **Causal mechanism:** Is there a plausible mechanism?
- **Correlation strength:** Does threat severity correlate with decline severity?
- **Exclusion of alternatives:** Can other threats be ruled out?

**Evidence hierarchy (strongest to weakest):**
1. **Experimental manipulation:** Threat removal leads to recovery
2. **Before-after control studies:** Decline follows threat onset
3. **Correlative studies:** Threat presence correlates with decline
4. **Expert inference:** Plausibility based on species biology

**Output template:**
```
## Causal Evidence Evaluation

### [Threat Name]
- **Evidence strength:** [Strong/Moderate/Weak]
- **Evidence type:** [Direct observation / Correlative study / Expert inference]
- **Causal mechanism:** [Description]
- **Correlation with decline:** [Strong/Moderate/Weak]
- **Alternative explanations ruled out:** [List]
- **Key studies/references:** [List]
```

### Step 4: Rank Threats by Causal Importance

**Weight threats by:**
- Evidence strength (40%)
- Mechanism plausibility (30%)
- Spatial-temporal correlation (20%)
- Exclusion of alternatives (10%)

**Output ranking:**
```
## Causal Importance Ranking

| Rank | Threat | Evidence | Mechanism | Correlation | Exclusion | Total Score |
|------|--------|----------|-----------|-------------|-----------|-------------|
| 1 | [Threat] | [Score] | [Score] | [Score] | [Score] | [Total] |
| 2 | [Threat] | [Score] | [Score] | [Score] | [Score] | [Total] |
...
```

### Step 5: Identify Threat Interactions

**Assess synergistic effects:**
- **Compounding risks:** One threat exacerbates another (e.g., habitat loss increases vulnerability to invasive species)
- **Tipping points:** Threshold effects where small changes lead to large impacts
- **Feedback loops:** Threats that reinforce each other (e.g., climate change → habitat loss → reduced resilience)

**Output template:**
```
## Threat Interactions
- **[Threat A] + [Threat B]:** [Description of interaction and impact]
- **[Threat C] + [Threat D]:** [Description of interaction and impact]
```

### Step 6: Identify Proximate vs. Ultimate Causes

**Distinguish:**
- **Proximate causes:** Direct mechanisms affecting individuals
- **Ultimate causes:** Underlying drivers creating the conditions for proximate causes

**Example:**
- Proximate: Predation by invasive rats
- Ultimate: Habitat fragmentation facilitating rat invasion

**Output template:**
```
## Proximate vs. Ultimate Cause Analysis

### Proximate Causes
1. **[Cause]:** [Description]
2. **[Cause]:** [Description]

### Ultimate Causes
1. **[Driver]:** [Description and link to proximate causes]
2. **[Driver]:** [Description and link to proximate causes]
```

## Evidence-Weighted Causal Ranking

### Scoring Framework

**Evidence Strength (40 points):**
- Strong (30-40): Direct observation, experimental manipulation
- Moderate (20-29): Correlative studies, expert elicitation
- Weak (10-19): Anecdotal, inferred from similar species

**Mechanism Plausibility (30 points):**
- High (25-30): Well-established mechanism, literature support
- Moderate (20-24): Plausible but less well-established
- Low (10-19): Speculative mechanism

**Spatial-Temporal Correlation (20 points):**
- High (15-20): Strong correlation in space and time
- Moderate (10-14): Moderate correlation
- Low (5-9): Weak or no correlation

**Exclusion of Alternatives (10 points):**
- High (8-10): Alternative threats strongly ruled out
- Moderate (5-7): Some alternatives ruled out
- Low (0-4): Alternatives not ruled out

### Ranking Process

**Step 1:** Score each threat across the four criteria

**Step 2:** Sum scores to get total causal importance score

**Step 3:** Rank threats by total score

**Step 4:** Flag uncertainties and data gaps affecting rankings

## Example Diagnoses

### Example 1: Amphibian Declines

**Species:** Mountain yellow-legged frog (*Rana muscosa*)

**Decline pattern:**
- **Temporal:** Sudden onset in 1970s-1980s, continuing
- **Spatial:** Widespread across Sierra Nevada, some refugia
- **Demographic:** All life stages affected
- **Magnitude:** >90% decline in many populations

**Threat inventory:**
- **Invasive species:** Trout present in lakes (predation on larvae)
- **Disease:** Chytrid fungus detected in populations
- **Climate:** Warming temperatures, reduced snowpack
- **Pollution:** Pesticide drift from agricultural areas
- **Habitat:** Livestock grazing in meadows

**Causal evaluation:**
- **Invasive trout:** Strong evidence (experimental removal leads to recovery)
- **Chytrid fungus:** Strong evidence (outbreaks correlate with mass mortality)
- **Climate change:** Moderate evidence (range shifts observed)
- **Pesticides:** Weak evidence (no clear correlation)
- **Grazing:** Moderate evidence (habitat degradation in some areas)

**Ranking:**
1. Chytrid fungus (primary driver)
2. Invasive trout (primary driver, synergizes with disease)
3. Climate change (secondary, increases disease susceptibility)
4. Grazing (localized impact)
5. Pesticides (unclear impact)

**Threat interactions:**
- Trout + Chytrid: Trout-resistant frog populations eliminated, leaving disease-vulnerable populations

**Management implications:**
- Prioritize disease mitigation and trout removal
- Maintain refugia populations
- Monitor disease dynamics

### Example 2: Coral Reef Decline

**Species:** Staghorn coral (*Acropora cervicornis*)

**Decline pattern:**
- **Temporal:** Gradual decline accelerating since 1980s
- **Spatial:** Caribbean-wide, severe in some areas
- **Demographic:** Reduced recruitment, increased mortality
- **Magnitude:** >80% decline throughout range

**Threat inventory:**
- **Climate change:** Ocean warming, acidification
- **Disease:** White band disease
- **Overfishing:** Loss of herbivorous fish
- **Pollution:** Nutrient runoff, sedimentation
- **Invasive species:** Lionfish (indirect effects)

**Causal evaluation:**
- **Climate change:** Strong evidence (bleaching events correlate with warming)
- **Disease:** Strong evidence (outbreaks cause mass mortality)
- **Overfishing:** Moderate evidence (altered ecosystem dynamics)
- **Pollution:** Moderate evidence (local effects)
- **Invasive lionfish:** Weak-moderate evidence (indirect effects)

**Ranking:**
1. Climate change (primary driver, underlies bleaching and acidification)
2. Disease (primary driver, interacts with thermal stress)
3. Overfishing (secondary, reduces resilience)
4. Pollution (localized)
5. Lionfish (localized, indirect)

**Threat interactions:**
- Warming + Disease: Thermal stress increases disease susceptibility
- Overfishing + Pollution: Reduces reef resilience to bleaching

**Management implications:**
- Prioritize climate mitigation and local resilience building
- Disease management where feasible
- Fisheries management to restore herbivore populations
- Water quality improvement
